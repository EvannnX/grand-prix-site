Driver prompts v1, and why there are two

  • Image-to-3D copies the pose in the picture. Tripo turns one image into one mesh, frozen in that pose, so each pose needs its own source image.
  • A natural pose breaks rigging. A hand holding a helmet fuses into the body in 3D. Auto-rigging needs limbs clearly apart from the torso, which is what a T-pose gives.
  • The two images need different lighting. The preview should look attractive; the 3D source needs flat light, no shadows and a white background, or shadows get baked into the texture.
  • They do different jobs. The preview is the character's poster; the T-pose is a neutral technical reference.

Order: generate the preview from the photo first, then the T-pose with both the photo and the chosen preview attached, so the 3D model matches the preview. Both prompts share the same style and outfit text, unchanged between drivers. The outfit carries no text, names or numbers.

Prompt 1 · Preview (natural pose)

Using the attached reference photo, create a chibi cartoon racing driver of this person for a racing game's character select screen.

Style: 3D cartoon character render, chibi proportions (about 2.5 heads tall), big head, small body, soft cel-shaded look with clean bold outlines, smooth simple shapes, saturated flat colors, toy-like and friendly. Keep the person's recognizable features from the reference photo (face shape, hairstyle, skin tone, and glasses if they wear them, drawn with thick simple frames), simplified and cute, never exaggerated or unflattering.

Outfit: racing suit in NYU violet (#57068C) with white side stripes, white gloves, black racing boots. No logos, no text, no names, no numbers anywhere.

Pose: confident, friendly pose in three-quarter view, one hand holding a violet racing helmet at the hip, the other hand giving a small thumbs-up, gentle smile.

Framing: full body, centered, plain light gradient background, soft studio lighting, no props other than the helmet, no text.

Prompt 2 · T-pose (source for Tripo and rigging)

Using the attached images — the photo for the person's identity, and the cartoon driver image for the exact style, colors and outfit — create the same chibi cartoon racing driver in a strict T-pose, as a reference image for image-to-3D conversion and rigging.

Style: 3D cartoon character render, chibi proportions (about 2.5 heads tall), big head, small body, soft cel-shaded look with clean bold outlines, smooth simple shapes, saturated flat colors, toy-like and friendly. Keep the person's recognizable features from the reference photo (face shape, hairstyle, skin tone, and glasses if they wear them, drawn with thick simple frames), simplified and cute, never exaggerated or unflattering.

Outfit: racing suit in NYU violet (#57068C) with white side stripes, white gloves, black racing boots. No logos, no text, no names, no numbers anywhere.

Pose: perfectly symmetrical T-pose, front orthographic view, facing the camera directly, arms straight out horizontally at shoulder height, palms facing down, fingers together, simplified hands, a clear gap between the arms and the body, legs straight and slightly apart with a visible gap between them, feet pointing forward, neutral closed-mouth expression.

Framing: full body from head to feet, centered, pure white background, flat even lighting, no shadows, no helmet, no props, no text, nothing overlapping or touching the body.

Driver 01: from photo to a rigged 3D model

The first driver went through the whole pipeline within about 40 minutes of finishing the prompts.

Cost: one model cost 55 Tripo credits and the rig 20; my balance went from 1,550 to 1,475.

My thinking at this step

Before tools like this, rigging meant opening the model in Blender, placing every bone by hand, and painting how much of the mesh follows each bone. Here it was one click on a preset. My work moved from building the skeleton to deciding which image is good enough to become the model, and preparing that image so the AI can rig it. The T-pose prompt exists only for that reason, and Tripo's own rig panel asks for exactly that.

Gap between prompt and output: the prompts asked for a cel-shaded look with bold outlines and flat colors. The images came back with photo-like faces on cartoon bodies and no outlines. Proportions, outfit and colors match.

A question these maps raise

The drivers are AI-generated and the tracks are human-made assets from the internet, so the game mixes two kinds of borrowed material. Inside the files, parts have pinyin-style names (for example map_daoju_02, where "daoju" reads like 道具, "item") and a built-in minimap mesh, a pattern common in levels extracted from an existing commercial kart game. The uploader labels them CC BY, but the pages do not say the uploader made them. I have not confirmed either way. This connects to the assignment's question of who owns the content: for Image 2 and Tripo it depends on the tools' terms; for these maps, on whether a free label on the internet can be trusted.

Shift 4: playtest feedback, and the fixes

ShiftThe 18:22 decision is reversed: Kart 2's mixed wheel parts are now split in code. The vote screen shows real renders of the maps instead of drawn outlines.

I played the first build and reported three problems:

  1. The wheels orbited an empty point instead of turning around their axles.
  2. On the space map the karts did not stick to the road.
  3. The map vote showed a drawn outline of the road, not what the map looks like.

1 · Wheels

The cause was the same thing noticed at 13:40: the bounding boxes saved in Tripo files are wrong, and three.js uses them to find a mesh's center. Claude measured every wheel center from the actual vertices and wrote them into the game. For Kart 2's right side it fitted a circle to the tire; those wheels turned out not to be mirror images of the left ones, and the game now spins only the triangles inside the tire's circle, leaving the fender on the body.

2 · Sticking to the road

The sky track has a stretch where the road mesh simply stops and resumes higher up (probably a jump pad in the original game), so the karts fell. Now karts follow the real road surface, and where the mesh has a gap they follow the racing line's height. In simulated races all four karts stayed on the road and finished on every track: Sky Plaza Climb in about 30 seconds, Flower Loop's 3 laps in about 92, Mountain Run in about 70.

3 · Map previews

The vote cards now show aerial renders of the actual maps, made with the same engine as the game.

This time Claude found a way to test on its own: it downloaded three.js through git and ran the game in a local browser, checking every screen at desktop and phone width and simulating whole races. Shift 6: collisions, smaller karts, and a closer camera

ShiftTwo 20:24 choices are reversed: the karts are drawn at half that size (0.675× instead of 1.35×), and the camera moves in close instead of further back.

I asked for three things at once: physical collisions between karts, with the maths worked out properly; karts half their current size; and a camera much closer to the kart, like KartRider or Mario Kart. The old view was too far away.

1 · Collisions

Until now, two karts that touched were just pushed apart and each lost 3% of its speed. Nobody bounced. Claude replaced this with a small physics model:

  • Shape. Each kart is a capsule on the ground: a line along its heading with a radius around it, sized from the measured wheel positions (not from the Tripo bounding boxes, which are wrong, see 20:24).
  • Weight. Each kart has a mass: Buggy 1.3, Roadster 1.05, Classic 1.0, Racer 0.9. A light kart that hits a heavy one comes off worse.
  • The hit. Overlapping karts are pushed apart by weight, then an impulse acts along the line between them: j = −(1+e)·v·n / (1/mA + 1/mB + rotation terms). The total momentum stays the same; about a quarter of the energy is lost (e = 0.35). A hit away from a kart's center makes it spin, and a side-swipe slows both karts through friction.
  • Small time steps. Physics runs 120 times a second, so fast karts cannot pass through each other between two frames.
TestBeforeAfterTotal momentum
Rear-end, Classic into Classic20 and 5 m/s9.9 and 15.1 m/s25.0 → 25.0
Light Racer into heavy Buggy20 and 5 m/s8.0 and 13.3 m/s24.5 → 24.5

The first version spun the karts too hard. A nearly straight rear-end hit set both karts turning at the maximum rate, because a kart only half a metre long resists turning very little. That is correct for the numbers, but the game's speeds are huge for that size. Claude added a "spin resistance" factor of 8, which brings that hit down to about 0.7 rad/s, a small wobble.

2 · Half size

Karts and drivers are now 0.675× instead of 1.35×. A Classic is about 0.5 m long and 0.43 m wide in the game world.

3 · The camera

The camera now sits 1.9 m behind the kart and 0.8 m up, aimed at a point 2.2 m ahead, so the kart sits low in the middle of the screen. It turns a little after the kart does, so corners swing the view, and the field of view widens from 66° to 78° at top speed to make speed visible.

The first try still looked far away when driving fast. Claude measured it: the camera was 3 m behind instead of 1.9. The cause was the smoothing, which follows the kart a fixed fraction per frame, so the faster the kart, the further the camera trails (about speed ÷ 12). The distance is now locked to the kart, and only the turning and the height are smoothed.

To make the "before" picture honest, Claude ran the original version from the first commit rather than imitating the old camera with the new code; its first attempt at imitating it did not look like the real thing.

Shift 7: drifting, KartRider style

ShiftCornering changes from grip driving to drifting. Every kart, the AI included, slides through the corners.

How a drift works

  • Starting. Hold Shift or Space while steering, above 8 m/s.
  • The slide. Until now a kart always moved the way it pointed. Now the kart's body and its direction of travel are separate: in a drift the body swings into the corner and the travel direction follows slowly, so the kart slides sideways, up to about 43°. Steering into the corner tightens the drift, steering out of it opens it. A drift costs a little speed.
  • The boost. A drift held for 0.7 s charges a level-1 mini-turbo, 1.4 s a level-2. Letting go fires it: a push of speed and a higher top speed for a moment. A gauge above the speedometer shows the charge.
  • Collisions and walls use the direction of travel, so a sliding kart hits things the way it is really moving.
  • The camera follows the direction of travel and shows part of the drift angle, so I can see the kart sideways.

The AI drifts too, after one fix

Opponents drift on any corner sharper than about 30° ahead of them. In the first test, on Flower Loop's hairpins, they bounced from wall to wall: sometimes they started a drift the wrong way while correcting their line, and they never let go of a drift that was turning too far. Now an AI kart only drifts in the direction the corner bends, and lets go as soon as it has to counter-steer hard.

Shift 10: driving on Sky Plaza's twisted road

ShiftKarts no longer move in a flat world with "down" always pointing down. They drive along whatever surface is under them, in 3D, and the camera tilts with them. Sky Plaza turns out to be a road that twists into a wall ride and ends upside down.

On the space map the camera stayed upright even when my kart was driving sideways, and the kart kept passing through the road at other heights and jumping from place to place. I asked for the camera to tilt with the road, and for Claude to work out again how a kart should travel on this map's curved surface. I also asked for the engine to be quieter than the music.

What the road really does

Claude measured the road's tilt at every point of the racing line. From point 145 the road banks up to 80°, almost a wall ride; at point 200 it passes vertical, and from point 230 to the finish it is upside down. The drawn side of the road mesh agrees: it faces down there. Two things followed from this:

  • The old physics could not drive it. It looked for the road straight down, kept karts upright and the camera vertical. On a road leaning 80° that finds the wrong spot or nothing, so karts jumped between levels, were put back on the track, and drove on the back of the upside-down road.
  • The "launch pad" at 23:40 was not a gap at all. It was the steepest part of the bank, where looking straight down misses the road. Measured along the road's own normal, Sky Plaza has no gaps.

How karts move now

  • Each kart has its own "up", the road's normal under it. Steering turns the kart around that axis, and the road is found along the kart's own "down".
  • Gravity pulls straight down on gently sloped road, and toward the road where it banks more than about 37°, as on an anti-gravity track. On the other maps nothing changes.
  • Walls, lanes and the grid are measured across the road's width instead of horizontally, so they bank with it.
  • Collisions use the same impulse maths as before, written with 3D vectors, and karts spin about their own up direction.
  • The camera keeps its place behind the kart in the kart's frame and gently follows the road's tilt.

The rewrite broke Mountain Run at first, and fixing it showed three more problems:

  • Climbing walls. Karts at the road edge took the steep side face of a curb as ground and climbed it. A face more than 45° off the road's normal no longer counts as ground.
  • Jumps in position. Right after a gap the walls close in from the line's full width to the measured road. Karts were pushed back 2–4 m in one frame; now they slide back in gradually. After the mascot update went online, the select screen sat at "Loading Qilin and the Classic" and never showed the kart. Later the track pictures on the track screen stayed empty too. I asked whether this was a browser problem, and said the game should have a cache that loads everything by itself.

Models and pictures come from there, with a quick background check for newer versions that costs a few bytes when nothing changed. The page and its scripts are checked online first so updates show at once, but if GitHub has not answered within three seconds the stored copy is used.

The walls were measured from the racing line, so where the line was wrong the walls were wrong too, and they stood across road I could see.

In the air it was the line again. The game keeps track of the nearest point on the racing line, and during a jump that point could switch to another stretch of the line passing nearby. Then three things acted on the kart: the jump help steered it toward that stretch's landing point, the line's height worked as a floor and hopped the kart up when it was below it, and the walls of that stretch pushed it sideways.

Walls from the map

On Mountain Run the walls are no longer measured from the racing line. A kart now hits what the map has:

  • The real faces of the rock, railings, tunnel walls and boards that stand on or beside the floor, tested at the kart's body height (4,899 faces). Anything lower than 22 cm above the wheels, like a curb or a seam, is driven over.
  • The floor's outer edges, where ground or a barrier continues past the road (1,412 pieces, worked out once and saved with the track).
  • No wall where the edge drops away: the cliffs over the sea, the sides of the springboards, the end of the road before the sea jump, and the steps of the wooden bridge, which comes down in drops of 0.5 to 1.4 m. A kart flies off there.

Off the road now means off that floor: a kart off it is slowed, and put back on the track if it stays off for 1.6 seconds. Flower Loop and Sky Plaza keep their old walls.

Jumps that are my own

My jumps are now only physics: no turn toward a landing point, no steering toward the line in the air, and no floor from the line's height. The nearest line point can only move on a few points while the kart is in the air. The one help left is at the sea jump, where the road ends, and only when I take it straight on.

The wooden chevron boards are springboards. Without anything extra, the board by the road loop threw the kart about 12 m at top speed, 1.5 m short of the plaza, and it fell into the gap. Now a kart leaving a board gets 6 m/s upward. The direction stays mine: in a simulated jump the kart's direction changed by at most 0.04° between frames in the air, and it landed in the plaza.

The racing line and the AI

Claude moved the racing line onto the floor with a path search over it: small cells, each step costing more the closer it runs to a wall, up a curb's height at most, down a step of up to 2 m. That changed the cave exit, the two corners, the path through the plaza (between the ramps instead of over one) and the start of the road loop. The AI's line now drives the road loop instead of the springboard shortcut, which is still there for me. Points that ran close to a wall were moved toward the middle. The race is now 986 m from start to finish, 21 m more than before.

The AI karts no longer drift on this map. With real edges, a drifting kart slid wide and over the drop; in the first test races that was nearly every fall. They also keep closer to their lane. A race with the AI driving my kart now takes about 131 seconds instead of 107, mostly because of the drifting and the fake jumps that are gone.

Finding the ground under a kart now uses the floor directly instead of casting a ray through the whole map, which made the physics three times faster.

Oct 27

Sky Plaza: air walls and the launch pad

Mountain Run was fine now. On the space station map there were air walls in many places on normal road. And where there is a launch pad on the road, it did not launch me; the kart went straight through the top of it.

The walls

Sky Plaza's road twists, all the way to upside down, so the Mountain Run approach (a floor that faces up) does not fit it. Its walls are still measured from the racing line, but they were only searched for up to the half-width recorded with the line, and the line wobbled by up to a metre and ran close to one side in places. The road is about 7 m wide between the railings almost everywhere, so on the far side of the line the wall stood in the middle of the road. From point 165 to 196, on the twisted straight with the launch pad, the right-hand wall had closed in to the 1.2 m minimum.

Claude now measures each side by walking outward across the road, in the road's own tilted plane, until the road and the map's hidden collision floor end, and saves the result with the track. Then it moved the racing line to halfway between the two walls and smoothed it, and measured the walls once more. 232 of the 556 sides moved outward by more than 30 cm in the first pass.

The launch pad

The pad is its own mesh lying on the road, and the game did not count it as road, so it was not solid and the kart sank into it. Now it is solid, and a kart that drives onto it is thrown off the road at 8 m/s. Because gravity on this twisted road pulls toward the road, the kart comes down on the road again: 1.7 m up, half a second in the air, about 6 m further on. The AI karts that cross it jump too.

Oct 28

Sky Plaza: the jump at the end of the lap

When I drove the space station track, the race ended by itself just before a jump. There is a floating stretch there, and you should be able to fly across it.

Sky Plaza is a circuit. After the ring that hangs upside down under the station, a short road sticks out into space: it curves down until it faces the same way as the side of the tall tower below it, and ends 35 m up, with a launch pad on its tip. After a gap of about 9 m the tower's face carries on, curves down 27 m and becomes the start straight. The racing line stopped where that short road begins, and the finish was put two points before the end of the line, so the race finished there.

Claude traced the stub and the tower along their surfaces and added them to the line (76 points, 56 m), with the gap between them as a jump. The track is now a one-lap circuit that ends at the start line, and the walls along the new part were measured the same way as the rest. Over the gap there are no walls, so nothing pushes a kart in the air.

My kart made the jump straight away: the pad on the tip throws it up, it is in the air for about half a second and lands at the top of the tower. The AI karts are a little slower there and dropped behind the tower's edge, so a launch pad in front of a gap now also gives the usual jump help toward the landing point.

Oct 28

Sky Plaza: finishing at the checkered line

It still did not finish at the line. I need to drive across the finish line before the race ends.

The start had been put at point 6 of the racing line, a default for tracks without a known start, which is just below the tower and nowhere near the finish line. Claude looked for the checkered pattern in the map's textures: it is a band in one of the atlas images, and the faces whose UVs use that band lie flat across the road inside the orange station building, 0.56 m past line point 85.

The start is now the first line point past it (point 86), so the grid lines up 3 to 8 m behind the checkered line and a lap only counts once a kart has driven over it. In a test race every kart finished between 0.2 and 0.5 m after the line. A lap still takes about 49 seconds.

Oct 28

How to play, and the chat records

The site's front page still showed the old drivers, the leaders' caricatures, in both pictures. They now show the four Qilin drivers in their karts and a race on Sky Plaza, rendered from the game itself.

I wanted a page that tells players how to play, before or right after they press Play, and I did not want the "As exported" look option, because it makes the game look unfinished. Play now opens a How to play screen first: the controls, drifting and the boost, the springboards and launch pads, being put back on the track, and what each track is. The title screen has a How to play button too. The look option is gone; the game always uses the cartoon shading.

I also wanted my chats with the AI tools at the top of this documentation: the Claude chat where the game design and PRD were worked out, the ChatGPT chat with the Image 2 workflow, and this Claude Code window. The first two are share links.
